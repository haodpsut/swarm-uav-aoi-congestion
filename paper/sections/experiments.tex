\subsection{Setup}
We simulate $K{=}60$ sensors uniformly placed in a square field (side length
varied from $5$ to $20$~km), a swarm of $M$ rotary-wing UAVs cruising at
$V{=}12$~m/s at altitude $H{=}100$~m, and a battery of about $67$~Wh with a
$15\%$ reserve. The charge time per port is $\tau_{\mathrm{charge}}{=}8$~min. The
propulsion power follows the rotary-wing model \cite{zeng2019energy}, and the
air-to-ground link uses a line-of-sight free-space rate. Candidate stations lie
on a $3{\times}3$ grid; unless stated otherwise the budget is $S_{\max}{=}2$
open stations and $C_{\mathrm{tot}}{=}4$ ports. In the joint comparison we use a swarm of $M{=}12$ UAVs. A reviewer asked us to
re-examine that choice, and the re-examination changed what we can claim.
Table~\ref{tab:mstar} gives $M^\star{=}12$ for a \emph{per-station} capacity of
$c{=}4$, i.e.\ for one station with four ports; the comparison setup instead splits
$C_{\mathrm{tot}}{=}4$ over up to two stations, which is not the same queue. Sweeping
$M$ directly under the actual placement and port split at the comparison field size
(Section~\ref{sec:permstar}) gives $M^\star{=}\rMstarOwn$, not $12$. We keep $M{=}12$,
which costs the proposed method $\rMstarPropCost\%$ relative to its own optimum, for
two reasons: every method peaks at the \emph{same} $M^\star{=}\rMstarOwn$, so the
choice favours none of them, and Section~\ref{sec:permstar} repeats the whole
comparison with each method at its own optimum and finds the ranking and the
magnitudes essentially unchanged. We therefore no longer describe $M{=}12$ as the
AoI-optimal swarm size, only as a fixed and neutral operating point. The scaling study in
Section~\ref{subsec:scale} covers genuinely large swarms, up to $M{=}60$, by
growing the charging capacity with the swarm. Every reported value is a mean over
$20$ to $30$ random sensor fields (fixed seeds), with the standard deviation
shown where relevant.

\paragraph*{Implementation, hyperparameters and runtime}
All results come from a \emph{single} machine, so no reported quantity mixes
hardware: a \rEnvGpu{} with \rEnvCores{} CPU cores, PyTorch~\rEnvTorch{} on
CUDA~\rEnvCuda{}, Python~\rEnvPython{}. The trajectory block minimizes
\eqref{eq:cetsp_loss} with Adam at learning rate \rHpLr{} and coverage weight
$\lambda_{\mathrm{cov}}{=}\rHpLambdaCov{}$: \rHpIters{} steps on the hover-point
positions, then \rHpReorder{} rounds that each reorder the visiting sequence by
nearest-neighbour plus 2-opt and polish it with $\lfloor\rHpIters{}/3\rfloor$ more
steps, \rHpSteps{} gradient steps in all. The budget is \emph{fixed}: no
early-stopping threshold and no random restarts, so a run is determined by its seed.
Each value averages \rRtSeeds{} seeds.

One solve takes \rRtLoTotal{}~s at $M{=}\rRtLoM{}$ and \rRtHiTotal{}~s at
$M{=}\rRtHiM{}$, and the split matters more than the total because the blocks scale
in opposite directions. The GPU trajectory block costs \rRtLoTraj{}~s and
\rRtHiTraj{}~s, barely growing with the swarm, since all UAVs are optimized in one
padded batch and a larger swarm means smaller sub-fields with fewer hover points
each; the CPU combinatorial block instead grows by almost an order of magnitude,
\rRtLoComb{}~s to \rRtHiComb{}~s, as the assignment search is re-scored per UAV. The
GPU thus accounts for \rRtLoGpuShare{}\% of a solve at $M{=}\rRtLoM{}$ but
\rRtHiGpuShare{}\% at $M{=}\rRtHiM{}$. Timing discards a CUDA warm-up call, synchronizes the device around each timer, and
is validated by requiring the two block times to sum to an independently timed solve.

Table~\ref{tab:params} lists every constant, including the rotary-wing power
coefficients and the link budget; it is generated by importing the values directly from
the artifact source, so it cannot drift from the code that produced the results.

\subsection{Queue-model validation against a discrete-event simulation}
\label{sec:des}
The whole analysis rests on the charging-queue model, so we first validate it.
We built a discrete-event simulation (DES) of the actual cycling UAVs, with a
first-in-first-served station and $c$ ports, in which each UAV alternates between
patrolling for a randomized operating time and traveling to the station to
recharge. The simulation supports both exponential and near-deterministic
operating and service times, and we run each configuration over eight seeds and
a long horizon after a warm-up, then measure the true mean queue wait.
Fig.~\ref{fig:des} compares three predictions against the DES. Under
matched assumptions (exponential operating and service), the finite-source
$M/M/c//N$ formula matches the DES to a mean relative error of $\rDesRelErr\%$
over the $\rDesFormulaN$ configurations in which the DES wait is resolvable, which
confirms the formula. For the realistic system (near-deterministic operating and
charging), the DES wait is lower, so the finite-source model is a
\emph{conservative upper bound} (it exceeds the real wait in
$\rDesConsHits/\rDesRealN$ configurations). In contrast, the open $M/M/c$ queue overpredicts the DES wait
by a mean factor of $\rDesOpenOver$, because it ignores the closed
population: only $M$ UAVs
exist, so the arrival rate falls as more of them are already charging. Using the
open queue therefore distorts the design decisions (we show below that it
produces a spurious placement crossover), which is why we adopt the
finite-source model throughout. The finite-source $M/M/c//N$ model assumes
exponential service and is thus a conservative bound: it exceeds the real wait by
a mean factor of $\rDesFiniteOverReal$. Replacing the exponential service by a deterministic-service
$M/D/c//N$ approximation, obtained with the standard service-variability
correction (the wait scales with $(1+c_s^2)/2$, where $c_s$ is the squared
coefficient of variation of the service time, so $c_s^2{=}0$ halves the wait),
tightens this to $\rDesMdOverReal\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:des}). The residual gap is due
to the near-deterministic \emph{operating} process, which a service-only
correction does not capture; since both models are upper bounds, the reported
AoI values remain safe rather than optimistic.

\subsection{Proposition 2: placement inversion}
\label{sec:prop2}
We compare, per instance, the coverage-optimal placement (minimum recharge
detour, contention-blind) against the traffic-optimal placement (which balances
the queueing load). The traffic-optimal placement lowers the peak AoI on
$\rGainStrictWins/\rSeeds$ seeds and ties on the remaining $\rGainTies$; it never
increases it. The mean reduction is $\rGainMean\%$ (median $\rGainMed\%$), and the
traffic-optimal solution selects \emph{different} station sites on
$\rSitesDiffer/\rSeeds$ seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:prop2}).

We are careful about what this does and does not establish.
Proposition~\ref{prop:inversion} is an existence result whose mechanism is a
\emph{per-station} port cap: because a single site cannot hold more than $c$ ports,
buying capacity requires opening a second site, and the proof therefore compares
configurations with different total capacity. The experiment above holds the total port
budget fixed and is thus not a test of that mechanism. It is a separate and, in this
respect, stronger empirical claim: the inversion appears even when no extra capacity is
bought. Section~\ref{sec:matched-ports} isolates it further. Neither result subsumes the
other, and we do not present the measurement as a verification of the proposition.

\subsection{Separating placement from installed capacity}
\label{sec:matched-ports}

A reviewer observed that the analytical comparison behind
Proposition~\ref{prop:inversion} contrasts one station holding $c$ ports with two
stations holding $c$ ports each, so that the second configuration enjoys both a
different placement and twice the installed capacity. We agree that the two effects
must be separated, and we do so here rather than by argument.

The experiment of Section~\ref{sec:prop2} already holds the \emph{total} port budget
fixed: both strategies receive the same $C_{\mathrm{tot}}$ and differ only in how they
distribute it. Across $\rSeeds$ seeds the traffic-driven placement lowers peak AoI by
$\rGainMean\%$ on average and is at least as good on $\rGainStrictWins$ of $\rSeeds$ seeds.
That comparison still allows the two strategies to allocate ports differently between
sites, so we split it further. On the $\rSamePortsN$ seeds where the two strategies choose \emph{identical} port allocations, and therefore differ only in \emph{where}
the stations stand, the traffic-driven choice is still better by $\rSamePortsMean\%$ on
average, with a median of $\rSamePortsMed\%$, and wins on $\rSamePortsWins$ of
$\rSamePortsN$. On the remaining $\rDiffPortsN$ seeds, where allocations do differ, the
mean gain is $\rDiffPortsMean\%$.

The effect therefore does not come from added capacity: it is, if anything, larger when
capacity is held identical in every respect.

Two caveats keep this honest. First, this is a measurement over $\rSeeds$ instances of
one scenario family, not a theorem: we have no proof that traffic-driven siting
dominates at matched capacity in general, and Proposition~\ref{prop:inversion} does not
supply one, because its argument runs through the per-station cap. Second, we retain the
cap in the statement of Proposition~\ref{prop:inversion} rather than restating the
proposition at matched capacity, because the cap is what makes the analytical comparison
tractable; the matched-capacity claim is empirical and is stated as such.

\subsection{Does the queue approximation change the ranking?}
\label{sec:ranking-md}

Section~\ref{sec:des} reports that the finite-source model is conservative and that the
gap widens under near-deterministic operation. The question that matters for the design
recommendation is not the size of that gap but whether it reorders the candidates. We
therefore repeat the placement comparison twice on the same seeds, once with the
exponential-service finite-source wait used throughout the paper and once with the
deterministic-service correction of Section~\ref{sec:des}, and compare the resulting
\emph{orderings}.

The coverage-driven placement is better on $\rRankCovWins$ of $\rSeeds$ seeds under
either model. On $\rRankTies$ seeds the two placements are exactly tied, and on
$\rRankFlip$ seed the deterministic-service correction reduces a narrow advantage to an
exact tie. No seed reverses the ordering. The reported gain is likewise stable: within
this single run, replacing the exponential-service model by the deterministic-service
correction changes the mean placement gain by $\rRankGainDelta$~percentage points. The recommendation and its magnitude are thus insensitive to the service-time
assumption over the range we can check, even though the absolute waiting times are not.

That test still scores every design with an \emph{analytic} wait, so it cannot rule out
a bias common to all of them. We therefore ran the stronger check a reviewer asked for:
one \emph{common} discrete-event simulator, with near-deterministic operating and
charging, applied to the \emph{final design} of all $\rDesNMethods$ methods. For each
returned configuration we simulate every open station with its own assigned UAVs and
port count, substitute the simulated wait for the analytic one in \eqref{eq:peak_m}, and
leave everything else unchanged; as a control, repeating the substitution with the
analytic wait reproduces the solver's own value to machine precision, so the simulator
is the only thing that differs. The analytic model is conservative for every method, by
$\rDesBiasLo$ to $\rDesBiasHi\%$, and the \emph{ordering is identical} under the two
evaluators. Under the common simulator the proposed design reaches $\rDesPropDes$~min
against $\rDesSingleDes$~min for the single pooled station, a gain of
$\rDesGainDes\%$, and it is the best design on $\rDesPropWins$ of $\rSeeds$ seeds. The
reported ranking is therefore not an artefact of the analytic surrogate.

\subsection{What the block decomposition costs}
\label{sec:joint-cost}

The formulation in~\eqref{eq:P0} is joint in placement, capacity, trajectory and
assignment. The solver is not: it partitions the field, solves the trajectory block
once, and then searches over placements and assignments. Since a station's position
changes the recharge detour, that ordering is an approximation and its cost should be
measured rather than asserted.

We measure it with one round of limited joint re-optimisation. Holding the sub-fields
and their patrol tours fixed, we reassign UAVs to sub-fields once the stations are
known, using an optimal assignment on the resulting detours, and then re-solve the
placement block. Averaged over $\rSeeds$ seeds the second round changes peak AoI by
$\rJointMean\%$, that is, it is indistinguishable from no change: it helps on
$\rJointBetter$ seeds, is slightly worse on $\rJointWorse$, and its largest
single-seed gain is $\rJointMax\%$. The ordering is therefore an approximation whose
cost we measure rather than assert, and that cost is negligible here.

\subsection{Why the objective is stated as an expectation}
\label{sec:metric-gap}

Section~\ref{subsec:aoi} defines the objective as the expected peak age, that is, the
average over cycles of the age reached just before each delivery. Two alternatives, a sample-path worst case and a time-average age, are ruled out by
measurement rather than preference; we report both here.

\paragraph{Expected peak against time-average}
For a sensor revisited every $T$ seconds the age rises linearly from zero to $T$, so the
expected peak is $\mathbb{E}[T]$ while the time-average is
$\mathbb{E}[T^{2}]/(2\,\mathbb{E}[T])$. The two therefore differ by a factor
$2/(1+\mathrm{CV}^{2})$. Building the cycle-length distribution from the simulated wait
samples, the measured ratio is $\rPaoiRatioLo$ to $\rPaoiRatioHi$ across
$\rPaoiConfigs$ configurations, since the cycle length varies little (coefficient of
variation at most $\rPaoiCvHi$). The two names are not interchangeable: a time-average would halve every AoI value
here.

\paragraph{Expected peak against sample-path worst case}

Using the discrete-event simulator of Section~\ref{sec:des} under near-deterministic
operation and charging, we record the full distribution of station waiting times rather
than its mean alone. Across the load points we examine, the $95$th percentile of the
wait exceeds the mean by a median factor of $\rPninefiveMed$, and the largest observed
wait exceeds it by a median factor of $\rMaxMed$. The spread is strongly regime
dependent: where the queue is saturated the mean is representative, with a
maximum-to-mean ratio of $\rMaxSat$, whereas in the lightly loaded configurations the
same ratio reaches $\rMaxHi$, because most excursions wait not at all and a few wait a
full service time.

The consequence is specific: a mean-based expression is an adequate surrogate for a
worst case exactly where the system is congested, and inadequate exactly where it is
well provisioned, the operating point Section~\ref{sec:guidelines} recommends. We therefore state the objective as an
expectation throughout, and we do not claim worst-case guarantees for the placements we
select.

\subsection{Feasibility, and where it stops holding}
\label{sec:feasible}
\label{sec:reach}

Constraint~\eqref{eq:P0e2} requires every UAV to keep enough energy to reach its
station. The solver re-evaluates it at every candidate, not once: the AoI evaluation
returns $+\infty$ when a UAV would exceed its one-way budget and the assignment
refinement calls it after each swap, so an infeasible configuration is never returned
\emph{when the constraint is active}. That qualification matters, and a reviewer's
request to report every constraint violation is what exposed it. The one-way budget is
$E_{\mathrm{res}}E_{\max}/P(V)=\rReachS$\,s of flight, i.e.\ $\rReachKm$\,km. With
$S_{\max}{=}2$ stations on the candidate grid, that budget is enough to serve the whole
swarm only in small fields. Table~\ref{tab:envelope} sweeps the field size: the
constraint is satisfied on all $\rReachSeeds$ seeds up to $L{=}\rEnvFullKm$\,km, starts
to fail at $\rEnvFirstFailKm$\,km, and admits no feasible configuration at all from
$\rEnvNoneKm$\,km onward. The comparison field of $\rEnvMainKm$\,km is past that point,
so the experiments there, and the field sweep beyond
$\rEnvFullKm$\,km, are run with \eqref{eq:P0e2} \emph{relaxed}; the largest one-way
overshoot at $\rEnvMainKm$\,km is $\rEnvMainViol$\,s of flight. At $L\le\rEnvFullKm$\,km the smallest slack anywhere is $\rReachMin$\,s with
$\rReachInfeasible$ infeasible configurations, but that figure does not describe the
large-field results, so we do not let it stand for the whole study. Two things follow.
The large-field AoI values hold \emph{under the assumption that a UAV can always reach
its station} and should be read that way; and the shortfall is a property of the
infrastructure, not of the placement algorithm: opening a third station restores feasibility up to $\rEnvThreeKm$\,km and a
fourth up to $\rEnvFourKm$\,km, but from $\rEnvHopelessKm$\,km no subset of the
$3\times3$ candidate grid suffices, because a corner sub-field is farther than
$\rReachKm$\,km from every candidate site. Serving such fields needs a denser candidate grid, a larger reserve, or in-field
charging, a provisioning statement Section~\ref{sec:guidelines} now carries. Every
other constraint of (P0) holds exactly on every returned configuration: station and
port budgets and open-station assignment show zero violation on all seeds, and the
collection-radius penalty leaves at most $\rCovViolMax$\,m residual. The separation constraint \eqref{eq:P0sep} is relaxed throughout, as
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:solver}.

\subsection{Joint solver versus baselines}
We compare the proposed joint solver (CETSP trajectory with traffic-optimal
placement) against: a single pooled station (all ports at one central station,
the strong Wei-style baseline \cite{wei2024}), a coverage-optimal placement, a
round-robin assignment, and an ablation that replaces the CETSP trajectory with
a nearest-neighbor patrol. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the ablation at a
$15$~km field: the proposed method attains the lowest peak AoI, the CETSP
trajectory contributes $\rAblEffect\%$ on average (bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval
$[\rAblCiLo,\rAblCiHi]\%$, per-seed range $\rAblMin$ to $\rAblMax\%$), and the
contention-aware placement contributes substantially more (about $18\%$ over the
coverage-optimal placement).

The single-UAV reference makes the premise of the paper concrete rather than rhetorical.
One UAV patrolling the whole field reaches a peak AoI of $\rOneUavAoi$~min against
$\rOneUavSwarmAoi$~min for the swarm, a reduction of $\rOneUavGain\%$ on average over
$\rOneUavSeeds$ seeds and at least $\rOneUavGainLo\%$ on every one of them
($\rOneUavLosses$ seeds favour the single UAV). This is the gap that motivates a swarm
in the first place, and it is also why the charging station becomes contended: the
swarm that closes this gap is exactly the swarm that must share the ports.

We state the trajectory contribution as an effect size rather than as a $p$-value, and
the reason is worth making explicit. The proposed solver beats the no-CETSP ablation on
all $\rAblWins$ of $\rAblPairs$ paired seeds, so an exact two-sided signed-rank test
returns $p=2/2^{\rAblPairs}=1.9\times10^{-6}$. That value is the \emph{floor} of the
test at this sample size: it is what any unanimous set of $\rAblPairs$ pairs returns,
regardless of whether the differences are large or negligible. It certifies the direction of the effect and nothing about its magnitude, so the
interval above carries the claim.

The same caution applies to the comparison as a whole. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} pits
the proposed method against $\rFamN$ baselines at once, so the tests form a family and
we report them as one: each is an exact two-sided paired signed-rank test, and after a
Holm correction across the family every comparison remains significant at
$p\le\rFamPHolmMax\times10^{-6}$. Because unanimity pins all five raw $p$-values to the
same floor, the informative quantities are the effect sizes, their intervals, and the
\emph{worst} seed rather than the mean. Against the single pooled station the mean gain
is $\rFamSingleMean\%$ (CI $[\rFamSingleCiLo,\rFamSingleCiHi]\%$) but only
$\rFamSingleWorst\%$ on the worst seed; against the coverage-optimal placement,
$\rFamCovMean\%$ (CI $[\rFamCovCiLo,\rFamCovCiHi]\%$) with a worst seed of
$\rFamCovWorst\%$. The method wins on every seed of every comparison, but on the least
favourable seed of the family the margin narrows to $\rFamWorstOverall\%$, and a reader
sizing a deployment should plan against that number rather than the mean.

\subsection{Scaling to a large swarm}

\subsection{Solver optimality}
\label{sec:optgap}
The two combinatorial blocks are heuristic in different ways, and it is worth
separating them. The placement and port search is \emph{exhaustive}, but only
over a finite candidate grid of sites and integer port splits; the
UAV-to-station assignment is a \emph{greedy} local search
(Section~\ref{sec:solver}). We therefore ask how far the pair together is from what we call the
\emph{conditional combinatorial optimum}: the optimum of the placement,
port-split and assignment subproblem \emph{conditional on} the per-UAV states
returned by the trajectory block. It is \emph{not} the optimum of $\mathrm{P0}$, which would also optimize the
trajectories. On small instances it is computable by enumerating
every open-site subset, every port split, and every UAV-to-station assignment; we
run our solver and the enumeration on identical per-UAV states, so the gap
measures only the combinatorial heuristic. Table~\ref{tab:optgap} reports the
result over $\rOptSeeds$ seeds. The returned configuration is \emph{exactly}
optimal for $M\le\rOptExactMax$, matching the enumerated optimum on all
$\rOptSeeds$ seeds; at $M=\rOptTopM$ it still matches on $\rOptTopHits$ of
$\rOptSeeds$ seeds and stays within $\rOptTopMean\%$ on average and
$\rOptTopMax\%$ in the worst case. The heuristic therefore matches this optimum wherever enumeration is tractable,
which supports its use at larger swarm sizes; it says nothing about the gap to
$\mathrm{P0}$ itself.

\subsection{Fairness of the fixed swarm size}
\label{sec:permstar}
Fixing $M$ for every method could favour the one that chose it, so we let each method
pick its own swarm size: we sweep $M$ over a grid at the comparison field size and
compare every method at \emph{its own} optimum. All five peak at the same
$M^\star{=}\rMstarOwn$, so no method is disadvantaged by a common $M$, and no
$M^\star$ sits at a grid boundary, so none of them is a censored extremum. At its own
optimum the proposed method reaches $\rMstarPropAtStar$~min against
$\rMstarPropAtBai$~min at $M{=}12$. The advantages survive the change almost exactly:
$\rMstarGainSingle\%$ over the single pooled station (versus
$\rMstarGainSingleBai\%$ at $M{=}12$), $\rMstarGainCov\%$ over the coverage-optimal
placement ($\rMstarGainCovBai\%$), $\rMstarGainRR\%$ over round-robin
($\rMstarGainRRBai\%$), and $\rMstarGainNoCetsp\%$ over the no-CETSP ablation
($\rMstarGainNoCetspBai\%$). The fixed swarm size is therefore not what produces the
reported gains.

\paragraph*{Extreme port scarcity}
The scaling study of Section~\ref{subsec:scale} grows the port budget with the swarm,
holding the UAV-per-port ratio constant, so it does not test contention. Here we hold the budget at $C_{\mathrm{tot}}{=}\rScarceCap$ ports while the swarm grows from
$M{=}\rScarceMLo$ to $M{=}\rScarceMHi$, so the ratio rises from $\rScarceRatioLo$ to
$\rScarceRatioHi$ UAVs per port. Peak AoI degrades from $\rScarceAoiLo$~min to
$\rScarceAoiHi$~min, a factor of $\rScarceFactor$, which is the congestion regime
Proposition~\ref{prop:ushape} predicts and the opposite of the capacity-matched
scaling. As a control we repeat the sweep with $C_{\mathrm{tot}}{=}M$, one port per
UAV, where contention cannot arise: peak AoI then \emph{decreases} monotonically with
$M$. The degradation is therefore caused by port contention, not by swarm size itself.

\subsection{Robustness}
Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} sweeps the main scenario parameters at $L=14$~km. The
gain of the proposed placement over the single pooled station is stable across
the collection radius ($11.2$ to $11.4\%$ for $r_c$ from $100$ to $400$~m) and
the charge time ($8.5$ to $13.5\%$). It is also larger, not smaller, on a
non-uniform field: when the sensors form clusters the gain rises to $19.0\%$,
because concentrated recharge traffic makes load balancing more valuable. The
advantage is therefore robust and, if anything, strengthens in realistic
non-uniform deployments.

\subsection{Discussion and design guidelines}
\label{sec:guidelines}
The results translate into concrete deployment guidance. First, do not size the
swarm by coverage alone: the peak AoI is non-monotone in $M$, so beyond $M^\star$
extra UAVs only add congestion, and Table~\ref{tab:mstar} shows $M^\star$ is set by
the installed capacity, so fleet and ports must be provisioned together. When
freshness targets tighten, adding ports is often more effective than adding UAVs to
an already congested system.

Second, size the station network to the energy reserve before optimizing its
placement. With a one-way budget of $\rReachKm$\,km, two stations serve a field only up
to $L{=}\rEnvFullKm$\,km; three reach $\rEnvThreeKm$\,km and four reach
$\rEnvFourKm$\,km, while beyond $\rEnvHopelessKm$\,km no subset of a $3\times3$
candidate grid suffices and the grid itself must be made denser
(Section~\ref{sec:reach}). Reachability is a feasibility gate that precedes any AoI
optimization.

Third, place stations to balance the queueing load, not only to minimize detour.
The coverage-optimal placement concentrates recharge traffic and inflates the wait;
spreading the stations lowers the peak AoI even at the cost of longer trips, and the
benefit grows with the field because travel then dominates. The field-size sweep makes the exception explicit: a single pooled station remains
sensible only in small fields, where travel is cheap.

Fourth, the queueing model matters as much as the optimizer: an open $M/M/c$ queue
overstates the wait by about $6\times$ and can flip a design decision, as the
spurious small-field crossover above shows. The finite-source model removes this bias and, being a conservative upper bound on
the near-deterministic reality, yields safe rather than optimistic estimates; it is
the default we recommend for swarm recharging with a bounded fleet.

Finally, the ablation isolates the gain: the contention-aware placement provides the
larger share, while the GPU close-enough-TSP trajectory
adds a smaller but consistent improvement by shortening the revisit period at a
realistic collection radius, and the joint solver combines the two without
violating the energy and budget constraints.
